Snow modeling

The results of the Snow Modeling paper indicate how different models perform better under different circumstances, and how calibrating a model, which is apparently not a common practice, can improve outputs. One of the models, the SNOW-17 model performs better in a shallow snowpack, whereas the VIC model performs better with deeper snowpacks. Additionally, the SNOW-17 model is in general more stable, but responds less to dynamic parameters. This makes is a less-than-ideal choice for climate modeling. In contrast, the VIC model contains more variables, and is more sensitive to a range of values, including albedo, meaning it is better suited to model unpredictable and varaible condition. This also means that careful calibration is required for optimum model performance.

Assignment Part 2

Recall our model of atmospheric conductance \[ C_{at} = \frac{v_m}{6.25*{ln(\frac{z_m-z_d}{z_0})}^2} \] \[ z_d = k_d*h \] \[ z_0 = k_0*h \]

\(z_m\) is the height at which windspeed is measured - must be higher than the vegetation (cm), it is usually measured 200 cm above the vegetation

\(h\) is vegetation height (cm)

\(v\) is windspeed (cm/s)

Typical values if \(k_d\) and \(k_o\) are 0.7 and 0.1 respectively (so use those as defaults)

F) Atmospheric Conductance Analysis

Comment on what this tells you about how atmospheric conductance and its sensitivity to variation in windspeed differs in this setting as compared to the setting that we examined in class where windspeed was lower and less variable and vegetation was taller.

Atmospheric conductance is most sensitive to windspeed, and second most sensitive to plant roughness. Increases in both correlate to an increase in conductance, and roughness is a better determinant of conductance than height. In class, with lower and less variable windspeeds, k_d was the biggest determinant of conductance. Second order interactions are farily limited in this scenario.
